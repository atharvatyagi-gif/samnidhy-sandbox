How a Stock Price Is Set

A share price isn't set by a company or an exchange. It's set by buyers and sellers posting orders, and a price only happens when one of them agrees to trade at the other's price. Below is a simplified order book for one of this session's stocks, starting from its real closing price. Pick a stock, then place buy or sell orders and watch what happens to the price.

Top gainer / top loser
The stocks whose price rose (or fell) the most in percentage terms during the last trading session, after our quality filters.
Trading session
One day of trading on the NSE, 9:15 AM to 3:30 PM IST on weekdays that aren't market holidays.
Order book
The live list of everyone currently willing to buy (bids) or sell (asks) a stock, and at what price.
Bid
The price a buyer is offering to pay. Shown in green.
Ask
The price a seller wants to receive. Shown in red.
Bid-ask spread
The gap between the best bid and best ask. See below for why it exists.
Market order
An order to buy or sell immediately at the best available price(s), rather than waiting at a chosen price.
LTP
Last Traded Price: the price at which the most recent trade actually happened.

This shows why a stock's price constantly moves: it's simply the price of the most recent trade, and every order that eats through the book pushes it up or down. A top gainer's whole day is thousands of buy orders like these, outpacing the sellers. The spread exists because the best ask is always a little above the best bid. That gap is how liquidity providers (traders who are always ready to buy or sell) get paid for the risk of holding shares while waiting for the next trade.

02 2

What a Company Is Worth

Two numbers investors use constantly: market capitalisation (how big the company is, in rupees) and the P/E ratio (how expensive its shares are, relative to its profit). Both below use the real, latest figures for this session's stocks.

What P/E means, concretely: if a company earns ₹10 per share over a year (its EPS, or earnings per share) and its stock trades at ₹200, its P/E ratio is 200 ÷ 10 = 20. That means investors are paying ₹20 today for every ₹1 of the company's annual profit.

Market Capitalisation

P/E Ratio · dashed line = median

Expensive or cheap? Ranked by P/E, relative to the group median

Market cap (share price × number of shares) measures a company's size, not its profitability. It says nothing about whether the stock is cheap or expensive. P/E is the opposite: it says nothing about size, but shows how much investors pay per rupee of profit. The taller bar is priced more expensively relative to its own current earnings, which can mean the market expects faster growth, or simply that the stock costs more for the same rupee of profit.

See how an assumed growth rate changes value

If a company's profit (and so its EPS) grows every year, and investors keep paying the same P/E multiple for it, its share price has to grow at the same rate to match. Drag the slider to assume a growth rate and see the implied price 3 years from now for each company, starting from its real current price and real current P/E.

Key terms in this lesson click to collapse
Market capitalisation
Share price × total shares outstanding: the stock market's current price tag for the whole company.
P/E ratio
Share price ÷ earnings per share (EPS). Shows how many rupees investors pay per rupee of annual profit.
EPS
Earnings per share: a company's annual profit divided by its number of shares.
Earnings growth rate
How fast a company's annual profit is assumed to increase, year over year.
Median P/E
The middle P/E of the stocks shown. Above it = relatively expensive within this group; below it = relatively cheap.
P/E "n/a"
Shown when there is no trailing P/E. This usually means the company made a loss over the last year, so the ratio has no meaning.

Key terms in this lesson click to collapse
Analysis period
The dates on which every stock shown and the Nifty 50 all have prices. All comparisons use this same period so they're fair.
Annualised volatility
How much a stock's daily returns typically swing, scaled up to a yearly figure. Higher means a bumpier ride.
Annualised return
The average yearly growth rate of the stock's price over the period, accounting for compounding.
Correlation
A number from -1 to +1 showing how closely two stocks' daily moves track each other.
Diversification
Holding several imperfectly-correlated investments together so their ups and downs partly cancel out, lowering overall risk.
Portfolio
The full collection of investments someone holds, and the weights (percentages) allocated to each.
Standard deviation
The statistical measure behind "volatility" here: how far values typically stray from their average.
Covariance matrix
A table of how much every pair of stocks moves together. Used to compute a portfolio's true risk.
Maximum drawdown
The biggest fall from a previous high to a later low over the period: the worst loss someone buying at the peak would have seen.
Beta
How much a stock tends to move when the Nifty 50 moves. 1 = in step with the market; above 1 = exaggerates its moves; below 1 = dampens them.

The Power of Compounding

A SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) means investing a fixed amount every month, whatever the market is doing. Each month's money starts earning returns, and then those returns start earning returns of their own. That snowball is compounding. Key terms in this lesson click to collapse
SIP
Systematic Investment Plan: investing the same amount at regular intervals (usually monthly) into a fund or stock.
Compounding
Earning returns on your past returns, not just on the money you put in. It makes growth accelerate over time.
Expected annual return
The yearly growth rate you assume. Defaults to the Nifty 50's real annualised return over the last 5 years. Past returns don't promise future ones, and one stock's past return is far less reliable than the market's.
Nifty 50
An index tracking 50 of the largest companies on India's National Stock Exchange: a common yardstick for "the market".
